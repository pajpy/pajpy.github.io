## Rockets and Spaceflight

Rockets are the machines that make space exploration possible. They are designed to lift spacecraft, satellites, scientific instruments, and astronauts beyond Earth’s atmosphere and into space.

Unlike airplanes, rockets do not need air to create motion. They work by pushing hot exhaust gases downward at high speed, which creates an upward force called **thrust**.

![Rocket launch](assets/images/launch.jfif)

## How Do Rockets Work?

A rocket engine burns propellant and produces hot gases inside a combustion chamber. These gases are forced out through a nozzle at very high speed. According to Newton’s third law of motion, the exhaust moving one way pushes the rocket in the opposite direction.

This is why rockets can work in space, where there is no air. They carry both fuel and an oxidizer, so they do not depend on the atmosphere to burn propellant.

### Why staging matters

Many launch vehicles are built in stages. Each stage contains its own engines and propellant. When a stage runs out of propellant, it separates from the rocket so the remaining vehicle becomes lighter and more efficient.

## The Main Parts of a Rocket

A full-scale rocket usually includes four major systems:

- **Structural system** – the outer body, tanks, and frame that hold everything together
- **Payload system** – the cargo carried by the rocket, such as satellites, probes, or crew capsules
- **Guidance system** – computers and sensors that help control the rocket’s flight
- **Propulsion system** – engines, fuel, oxidizer, pumps, and nozzles that produce thrust

These systems must work together very precisely because launches involve extreme heat, vibration, speed, and pressure.

## Saturn V: A Historic Moon Rocket

The **Saturn V** is one of the most famous rockets in history. NASA built it for the Apollo program, and it was the rocket that sent astronauts toward the Moon. It was a heavy-lift launch vehicle and remains one of the most important engineering achievements of the twentieth century.

Saturn V was also used to launch **Skylab**, America’s first space station. Its legacy is strongly connected with lunar exploration and the success of the Apollo missions.

![Saturn V](assets/images/saturnv.jfif)

### Why Saturn V is important

- It launched Apollo missions to the Moon
- It became a symbol of the Space Age
- It demonstrated the power of multi-stage heavy-lift rockets

## Space Launch System (SLS)

NASA’s **Space Launch System**, or **SLS**, is the agency’s modern deep-space rocket for Artemis missions. NASA describes it as a super heavy-lift rocket and states that it can send the Orion spacecraft, astronauts, and large cargo directly to the Moon in a single launch.

SLS represents the next generation of large exploration rockets and is a key part of future human missions beyond low Earth orbit.

![SLS rocket](assets/images/sls.jfif)

### What makes SLS important

- It is designed for deep-space exploration
- It supports the Artemis lunar program
- It can carry both crew and large cargo toward the Moon

---

## Falcon 9 and Reusable Rockets

One of the biggest changes in modern spaceflight is the rise of reusable launch systems. **Falcon 9**, developed by SpaceX, is a two-stage rocket designed to transport people and payloads into Earth orbit. It is well known for the reusability of its first stage, which can return and land after launch.

Reusable rockets are important because they can reduce launch costs and allow more frequent missions.

![Falcon 9](assets/images/falcon9.jfif)

### Why reusability matters

- It lowers the cost of launching payloads
- It makes launch operations more efficient
- It changes how often rockets can be flown
